Five problems worth describing

Identity & platform architecture

One identity layer, eighteen applications

Problem

A national grocery retailer had grown a set of internal applications — warehouse, store operations, HR, contracts, BI, communication — each with its own login and its own idea of who a user was. Every new application meant re-implementing authentication, and permissions drifted apart between them.

Approach

  • A central authentication and authorisation service as the single source of identity.
  • Office staff recognised through Active Directory integration — no password prompt at all.
  • A separate badge-and-password path for shop-floor terminals outside the domain.
  • Short-lived tokens for application-to-application handoff, so a user never re-authenticates when moving between systems.
  • Permission trees defined per service from a template, rather than hard-coded per application.
  • Roles derived from the org chart itself: if the directory says people report to you, the system treats you as their approver.

Outcome

Eighteen applications behind one identity and one permission model, served under a single domain with path-based routing. Adding an application became a matter of registering a service and declaring its permission tree — no new authentication code, and no new place for permissions to go stale.

Point of sale & regulatory compliance

A till that satisfies a national tax authority

Problem

Point-of-sale software has to register every sale with the state in a form that can later be audited. Certification is not a feature — an uncertified till cannot legally take money. At the same time a shop cannot stop selling because a network link dropped.

Approach

  • Designed the transaction path so fiscal registration is part of the sale, not a batch job bolted on afterwards.
  • A local offline queue keeps the till trading when the link to the fiscal service is unavailable, then reconciles on reconnect.
  • Reconciliation between the till's local database and the central ERP, with differences surfaced and correctable rather than silently lost.
  • A second-generation build targeting Android kiosk hardware alongside Windows.

Outcome

A certified commercial POS product running in live retail, and — because I own the product — end-to-end responsibility for it: architecture, licensing system, installer, printing service, and the fiscal certification itself.

Why this is relevant in Germany. This is structurally the same problem as TSE / KassenSichV: a certified security element in the receipt path, an offline mode that cannot lose transactions, and an audit trail that has to hold up years later. The regulation differs; the engineering does not.

Systems integration

Making systems talk that were never meant to meet

Problem

A quick-service restaurant operator ran point of sale on one vendor's Oracle stack, finance and inventory on a second vendor's PostgreSQL ERP, and procurement in SAP. Sales, goods receipts and stock movements had to cross all three — and were crossing them by hand.

Approach

  • Small, single-purpose services rather than one integration monolith: a POS-to-ERP sales sync running as a Windows service; a REST gateway that accepts goods-receipt pushes from SAP and writes ERP delivery notes; a receipt sync module.
  • All three on one shared template, so the fourth flow is a module rather than a new system.
  • Compiled with Native AOT — instant start, no runtime installed on the host, a smaller attack surface, and a binary that does not casually decompile.
  • Production databases treated as read-only until a write path is explicitly reviewed and approved.

Outcome

Three production integration services on a common Clean Architecture template. Manual re-keying between the three systems removed for the flows that were migrated, and each new document type became a bounded, testable addition.

Legacy migration

Retiring a spreadsheet that had become infrastructure

Problem

In-store product placement across 150+ stores — which brand sits on which stand, under which contract, checked physically each month — ran on a stack of spreadsheets, scripts and shared drive folders. It worked until it was load-bearing: no single source of truth, nothing enforced, no way to prove a check had happened.

Approach

  • Made the store floor plan the interface: placements are pins on the plan, moved by dragging, with zoom and pan.
  • The monthly physical check became a guided flow with photo evidence and an approval step, so a completed check is a record, not a claim.
  • Automatic alerts for incomplete placements and contracts approaching expiry — the two failure modes the spreadsheet could not see.
  • Field-to-office messaging in the same application, so the context of a problem stays attached to the placement it concerns.

Outcome

The process moved onto a system with enforced rules and an audit trail, while field teams kept working through the transition. The migration risk — not the feature list — was the hard part, and it is the part I would want to be asked about.

Applied AI

AI as a working part of the product

Problem

Two different questions. First: an ERP holds everything a business knows, but an AI assistant cannot reach any of it. Second: a bakery distribution business decides every morning how many loaves each van carries to each customer — too few loses a sale, too many becomes waste by evening.

Approach

  • An MCP server exposing ERP operations to AI agents, so an assistant can query and act on real business data instead of guessing.
  • A daily order-planning engine that recommends an exact quantity per rep, customer, product and date — and writes a one-sentence explanation in the operator's own language, so the number can be argued with.
  • A newsvendor model over the demand distribution, with ABC customer profiling, product shelf life, and damped feedback calibration against what actually sold.
  • One rule is deliberately asymmetric and enforced in code: never recommend below what already sold, and actively reduce returns. A symmetric "don't change much" rule would have been easier and wrong.

Outcome

A forecasting service in production with a covering unit-test suite, and an ERP that an AI agent can operate. I also use AI-assisted development daily — as a reviewer of its output, not a passenger.

04 — How I work

Four things that are true about me

I own the whole thing

On my own product that meant architecture, licensing, installer, printing service, typeface and the fiscal certification. I am comfortable being the person responsible when something is unclear, and I would rather ask a blunt question early than deliver a confident guess.

I migrate carefully

Most of my work replaced something that was already running a business. The interesting engineering is rarely the new feature — it is keeping the old path alive until the new one has earned the traffic.

I say what I don't know

My depth is .NET, SQL Server and integration. I am not going to claim deep cloud infrastructure or platform engineering that I have not run in production. What I do have is a long record of learning a system well enough to be trusted with it.
